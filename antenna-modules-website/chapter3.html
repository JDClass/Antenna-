Topic 1 of 8 · wire antennas

Long-wire antenna

A straight wire several wavelengths long. Open at the far end it carries a standing wave; terminated in a matched load it carries a travelling wave.

Working principle

  1. An RF current flows along a straight wire several wavelengths long. Every short piece of the wire radiates like a tiny (Hertzian) dipole, and the far field is the phasor sum of all these pieces.
  2. Open end (resonant): the wave reflects at the far end. The forward and reflected waves form a standing wave, so lobes appear symmetrically toward both ends: the pattern is bidirectional.
  3. Matched load (non-resonant): the load absorbs the wave, so only one travelling wave exists. Its phase lags along the wire at almost the speed of light, so the contributions add in phase in a cone tilted toward the load: the pattern is unidirectional.
  4. The longer the wire, the closer this cone moves to the wire axis. No current element radiates along its own axis, so the field is zero exactly at θ = 0°.
  5. V and rhombic antennas tilt two or four such wires so that their main cones overlap in one direction.

Topic 2 of 8 · wire antennas

Loop antenna

A small loop behaves like a magnetic dipole. Its radiation resistance is tiny, which is why small loops are mainly receiving antennas.

Working principle

  1. A loop carrying a current I encloses an area A, so it is a magnetic dipole with moment m = N·I·A.
  2. When the current alternates, the magnetic moment alternates and radiates. By duality, the far field is the same as a short electric dipole's with E and H swapped: E is along φ, in the plane of the loop.
  3. Every point of a small loop is close to the same distance from a far observer, and opposite sides carry opposite currents. Their fields almost cancel, which is why Rr is tiny and grows as (size)⁴.
  4. Receiving (Faraday's law): the wave's changing magnetic flux through the loop induces a voltage. Flux is maximum when the wave arrives in the plane of the loop and zero when it arrives along the axis, so turning the loop finds a sharp null for direction finding.
  5. More turns or a ferrite core raise the flux linkage, and so the induced voltage and Rr.
Small loop (C < λ/10): E_φ ∝ sinθ
R_r = 20π²(C/λ)⁴ · N² Ω
Any size, uniform current: E_φ ∝ J₁(ka·sinθ)
  • The small loop is the dual of the short dipole: same doughnut pattern, D = 1.5 (1.76 dBi), but E and H swap roles. The null is along the loop axis.
  • Rr grows as the fourth power of size. A loop of C = 0.1 λ has Rr ≈ 0.02 Ω, far below the wire's loss resistance, so efficiency is low.
  • N turns multiply Rr by N². A ferrite core multiplies it by μeff². Both are used in AM receivers.
  • Loss resistance: R_L = N·(C/2πb)·R_s, with Rs = √(πfμ₀/σ).
  • The sharp null along the axis makes small loops good for direction finding.
  • A loop with C ≈ λ is resonant. Its current is no longer uniform and its maximum is along the axis.

Topic 3 of 8 · wire antennas

Helical antenna

A wire wound as a helix over a ground plane. When one turn is about one wavelength around, it fires a circularly polarized beam along its axis.

Working principle

  1. A helix combines a loop (each turn) and a dipole (the axial advance per turn). Which behaviour dominates depends on the size of a turn relative to λ.
  2. Normal mode (C ≪ λ): each turn acts as a small loop giving E_φ and a short dipole giving E_θ. Both peak broadside and are 90° out of phase, so the polarization is elliptical. It becomes circular when the two are equal.
  3. Axial mode (C ≈ λ): a travelling wave goes round each turn. One wavelength of current around a turn makes the field rotate once per cycle, giving circular polarization along the axis.
  4. The N turns, spaced about λ/4, form an end-fire array along the axis. The wave on the wire slows down by itself so that the turns add in phase along the axis, close to the Hansen–Woodyard condition, which gives a sharp beam.
  5. The ground plane reflects backward radiation forward. The winding sense sets RHCP or LHCP.

Topic 4 of 8 · VHF and UHF

Yagi–Uda antenna

One driven dipole, one reflector behind it and a row of directors in front. Only the dipole is fed; the others are excited by coupling. This simulation solves the real wire currents.

Working principle

  1. Only the driven dipole is connected to the transmitter. Its field induces currents in the nearby parasitic elements (mutual coupling), and they re-radiate.
  2. The phase of an induced current depends on the element's own reactance. An element longer than resonance is inductive: its current lags and it acts as a reflector. One shorter than resonance is capacitive: its current leads and it acts as a director.
  3. With spacings of about 0.1–0.4 λ, these phase shifts plus the travel time between elements make the re-radiated fields add in phase toward the directors and cancel toward the reflector.
  4. The row of directors works as a slow-wave guiding structure: the wave travels along it and leaves from the far end as an end-fire beam. Each extra director adds a little gain.
  5. The parasitic elements also lower the driven element's input impedance, which is why a folded dipole is often used.
  • Driven element: about 0.45–0.49 λ, often a folded dipole to raise the low input impedance.
  • Reflector: slightly longer than λ/2. Its current is inductive and lags, so it pushes radiation forward. One reflector is enough; more add little.
  • Directors: 0.38–0.45 λ, shorter than λ/2. Their capacitive currents lead, guiding the wave forward like a slow-wave structure.
  • Spacing: reflector 0.15–0.25 λ, directors 0.2–0.4 λ.
  • Gain grows with the number of directors, but each extra director adds less: 3 elements ≈ 7–8 dBi, 15 elements ≈ 14–15 dBi.
  • Typical bandwidth is a few percent. Widely used for TV reception, amateur radio and point-to-point VHF/UHF links.

How the simulation works

Each element is split into short segments with piecewise-linear current, and Hallén's integral equation is solved for the segment currents with the method of moments (wire radius 0.0034 λ). The pattern, gain, front-to-back ratio and input impedance are computed from those currents.

Topic 5 of 8 · frequency-independent antennas

Spiral and log-periodic antennas

If an antenna's shape is defined by angles alone, it looks the same at every frequency. Real antennas are cut off at an inner and outer size, which set the band edges.

Working principle

  1. Rumsey's principle: pattern and impedance depend on size in wavelengths. If the shape is defined only by angles, scaling the frequency just rescales the antenna into itself, so its behaviour does not change.
  2. A real antenna has to stop somewhere. It still works over a wide band because, at each frequency, only an active region of the right electrical size radiates. Current falls off quickly beyond it, so cutting the structure there has little effect.
  3. Spiral: two arms carry currents outward. Where the circumference is about one wavelength, currents at opposite points of the arms come into phase and radiate. That active ring is a one-wavelength loop with a travelling wave, so the radiation is circularly polarized.
  4. Log-periodic dipole array: dipoles of geometrically scaled length are fed by a criss-crossed (transposed) line. The 3–5 dipoles near λ/2 resonate and radiate. Shorter ones are inactive, and the longer ones behind act as reflectors, so the beam points toward the short end.
  5. Changing frequency moves the active region along the structure. Multiplying f by 1/τ shifts it by exactly one element, so the properties repeat periodically with log f.

Rumsey's principle

Pattern and impedance depend on the antenna's size in wavelengths. A structure specified only by angles has no characteristic length, so its performance is independent of frequency. Only a small active region, where the structure is about the right size, radiates at any one frequency.

Topic 6 of 8 · aperture antennas

Pyramidal horn antenna

A waveguide flared into a larger aperture. A wider flare gives more gain until the curved wavefront across the mouth causes phase error.

Working principle

  1. An open-ended waveguide radiates poorly: its small aperture gives a broad beam, and the sudden change from guide impedance to free space (377 Ω) reflects much of the power.
  2. Flaring the walls gradually transforms the guide into free space, so reflections fall and the aperture grows, giving a narrower beam and more gain.
  3. By Huygens' principle, each point of the aperture acts as a secondary source. The far field is the Fourier transform of the aperture field.
  4. Inside the horn the wave spreads from the apex, so the wavefront at the mouth is curved, not flat. Edges lag the centre in phase by the error s (E-plane) or t (H-plane).
  5. For a given length, widening the mouth first adds gain, then the phase error starts to cancel it. The optimum horn sits at that turning point.
  • The TE₁₀ field in the aperture is uniform in the E-plane and cosine-shaped in the H-plane.
  • Because the wave spreads from the apex, the aperture phase is quadratic. Maximum phase error at the edge, in wavelengths:
E-plane: s = b₁² / (8λρ₁)
H-plane: t = a₁² / (8λρ₂)
  • For a fixed length there is an aperture size that gives the most gain. Beyond it, phase error cancels the extra area.
  • Optimum horn: b₁ = √(2λρ₁) (s = 1/4), a₁ = √(3λρ₂) (t = 3/8). Aperture efficiency ≈ 51 %.
D = (4π/λ²)·ε_ap·a₁·b₁
HPBW_E ≈ 56° · λ/b₁ , HPBW_H ≈ 67° · λ/a₁
  • Horns are used as gain standards, as feeds for reflectors and in radar and test ranges. They are wideband, robust and easy to predict.
  • E-plane and H-plane sectoral horns flare in one plane only.

Topic 7 of 8 · aperture antennas

Parabolic reflector antenna

A feed at the focus illuminates a paraboloid, which turns the spherical wave into a plane wave across a large aperture.

Working principle

  1. A parabola has the property that every ray from its focus reflects parallel to its axis.
  2. Equivalently, the path from the focus to the reflector and on to the aperture plane is the same (2f) for every ray. The spherical wave from the feed therefore leaves as a plane wave with uniform phase across the whole dish.
  3. A large in-phase aperture radiates a narrow, high-gain pencil beam, with gain proportional to (D/λ)².
  4. The feed pattern sets how strongly each part of the dish is illuminated. A broad feed spills power past the rim; a narrow feed leaves the outer area weakly used. Good designs balance spillover against taper.
  5. Reciprocity: in reception, the dish collects a plane wave and focuses it onto the feed.
  • All paths from the focus to the aperture plane via the dish have equal length, so the aperture is in phase.
  • Subtended half-angle: θ₀ = 2·tan⁻¹[1/(4·f/D)].
G = ε_ap · (πD/λ)²
ε_ap = ε_spillover × ε_taper (× phase, blockage …)
HPBW ≈ 70° · λ/D
  • Spillover: feed power that misses the dish. A broad feed or deep dish spills less.
  • Taper: a feed that is weaker at the rim illuminates the aperture unevenly, wasting area.
  • The two pull in opposite directions. The best compromise is an edge illumination of about −10 to −11 dB, giving ε_ap ≈ 80 % in theory. Real dishes reach 55–70 %.
  • Feed model here: G_f(θ') = 2(n+1)·cosⁿθ'. Larger n is a narrower feed.
  • Variants: Cassegrain (hyperbolic sub-reflector) and Gregorian, which put the feed behind the dish, and offset-fed dishes, which remove blockage.

Topic 8 of 8 · microwave

Rectangular microstrip patch antenna

A metal patch on a grounded dielectric substrate. It radiates from the fringing fields at its two open edges, which act like two slots half a wavelength apart.

Working principle

  1. The patch and ground plane form a thin cavity, closed by electric walls (top and bottom) and open magnetic walls at the edges.
  2. At the dominant TM₀₁₀ resonance the patch is about half a guided wavelength long. The field under it varies as cos(πy/L), so it points up at one end and down at the other.
  3. At the two open ends the field fringes out beyond the edges. The vertical parts are opposite and cancel, but the horizontal parts point the same way and add, so the patch radiates broadside.
  4. The patch is therefore equivalent to two radiating slots of width W and height h, half a wavelength apart and fed in phase. The side edges along L contribute almost nothing.
  5. The cavity stores much more energy than it radiates per cycle (high Q), which is why the patch is narrowband. A thicker, lower-εr substrate lowers Q and widens the band.

Transmission-line model design

W = (c / 2f₀)·√(2/(εr + 1))
ε_eff = (εr+1)/2 + (εr−1)/2 · (1 + 12h/W)^{−1/2}
ΔL = 0.412h (ε_eff+0.3)(W/h+0.264) / [(ε_eff−0.258)(W/h+0.8)]
L = c / (2f₀√ε_eff) − 2ΔL
  • Fringing fields make the patch look longer by ΔL at each end, so the physical length is a little under λg/2.
  • Edge resistance R_in = 1 / [2(G₁ + G₁₂)] is typically 150–300 Ω. An inset feed lowers it: R_in(y₀) = R_in(0)·cos²(πy₀/L).
  • Bandwidth is narrow, a few percent. It grows with thicker substrate and lower εr (Jackson: BW ≈ 3.77·(εr−1)/εr² · (W/L)·(h/λ₀)).
  • Directivity is about 6–8 dBi. Feeds: microstrip line, inset, coaxial probe, aperture coupling, proximity coupling.
  • Low profile, cheap, easy to integrate with circuits and to build into arrays. Used in phones, GPS, Wi-Fi and 5G.
