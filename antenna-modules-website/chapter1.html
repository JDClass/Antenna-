Part 1 · Introduction

What is an antenna?

An antenna is a transducer. It converts a guided wave travelling on a cable or circuit trace into an electromagnetic wave travelling through free space, and back again. IEEE Std 145 defines it as the part of a transmitting or receiving system designed to radiate or receive electromagnetic waves.

How it radiates

Radiation needs accelerating charge. A time-varying current on a conductor, such as the sinusoidal current on a dipole, launches fields that detach and travel outward at the speed of light. The antenna works best when its size is comparable to the wavelength.

λ = c / f

Reciprocity

A passive antenna made of linear materials behaves the same way when transmitting and receiving. Its pattern, gain, impedance and polarization are identical in both modes, so you can measure an antenna in whichever mode is easier.

Gtx = Grx

Field regions

Close to the antenna, stored (reactive) energy dominates. Further out the pattern still changes with distance (radiating near field). Beyond about 2D²/λ the pattern stops changing shape. This far field is where pattern, gain and directivity are defined.

Rff = 2D² / λ

The frequency range where a specification holds, most often S11 ≤ −10 dB. Inversely related to the quality factor Q.

The orientation and rotation of the electric field: linear, circular (RHCP/LHCP) or elliptical. Mismatch costs power.

Effective length

The length that, multiplied by the incident field, gives the open-circuit voltage. A half-wave dipole has le = λ/π.

Effective area

The area over which a receiving antenna captures power: Pr = W·Ae. Larger gain or longer wavelength means larger area.

Antenna temperature

The noise temperature the antenna delivers, set by what its pattern sees (sky, ground) and its own loss.

Received power on a one-way free-space link. Falls as 1/R², so 6 dB per doubling of distance.

Radar range equation

Two-way path with a target of radar cross section σ. Falls as 1/R⁴, so 12 dB per doubling of range.

Reciprocity

Transmit = receive

Pattern, gain, impedance and polarization are the same in both modes, so every parameter above applies to receiving antennas too.

Watch for

  • Ten times the frequency gives one tenth the wavelength and one tenth the antenna.
  • Raising εr shrinks the patch by 1/√εr. The dipole in air does not change.

Watch for

Watch for

  • Γ = (Z − Z₀)/(Z + Z₀). Only Z = Z₀ gives Γ = 0, VSWR = 1 and no reflection.
  • The standard dipole (73 + j42.5 Ω) is not resonant. Shortening it slightly removes X and leaves about 70 Ω.

Near resonance an antenna behaves like a series RLC circuit: Z(f) = R + jRQ(f/f₀ − f₀/f). The plot shows S11 against frequency. The shaded band is where S11 ≤ −10 dB.

Watch for

  • Double Q and the bandwidth halves: BW ≈ (VSWR − 1)/(Q·√VSWR).
  • Move R away from 50 Ω: the dip gets shallower and the band shrinks, then vanishes when S11 at f₀ is worse than −10 dB.
  • The Chu limit Q ≥ 1/(ka)³ + 1/(ka) ties Q to the radius a of the sphere enclosing the antenna. A tiny antenna cannot be wideband.

SIMULATION 5

Polarization and polarization loss

The tip of the electric-field vector traces a line (linear) or a circle (circular), viewed along the direction of travel. The receiving antenna collects only the part of the field that matches its own polarization.

90° = vertical, 0° = horizontal

Polarization loss

Watch for

  • Linear to linear: PLF = cos²Δψ. 45° costs 3 dB, 90° ideally blocks everything.
  • Circular to linear always costs 3 dB, whatever the tilt. Satellites use circular polarization so a rotating receiver never falls into a null.
  • Same-hand circular is a perfect match. Opposite hands are fully cross-polarized.

Watch for

  • Double the distance: Pr falls by 6 dB. Ten times the distance: 20 dB.
  • Raise the frequency with fixed gains: path loss grows, because each antenna's aperture shrinks as λ².
  • One extra dB of antenna gain buys exactly one dB of margin, the same as one dB of transmit power.
SIMULATION 7

Field regions, radiation power density and radiation intensity

Power density W (W/m²) spreads over a growing sphere and falls as 1/r². Radiation intensity U = r²·W (W/sr) removes that spreading, so in the far field it depends only on direction. Directivity is D = Umax/U0 with U0 = Prad/4π.

1 cm to 10 km, log scale

Watch for

  • Double r: W drops to one quarter (−6 dB) but U does not change at all.
  • Umax/U0 is always the directivity you set. That is the definition of directivity.
  • The far-field boundary 2D²/λ grows with the square of the antenna size and with frequency. A large dish at 12 GHz needs hundreds of metres.
  • For antennas smaller than λ the boundaries become R₁ ≈ λ/2π and R₂ ≈ λ (rule-of-thumb values).

Watch for

  • Half-wave dipole: le = λ/π ≈ 0.318 λ. Short dipole with triangular current: le = l/2.
  • A short dipole has almost the same effective area as a half-wave dipole (0.119 λ² against 0.130 λ²) but a tiny radiation resistance, so it needs careful matching.
  • TA = η·TB + (1 − η)·Tp. Loss in the antenna adds noise from its own warm metal, which matters most when it looks at cold sky.
  • Lower the frequency at fixed gain: λ² grows, so Ae and Pr grow.

SIMULATION 9

Radar range equation

A monostatic radar sends a pulse to a target and listens for the echo with the same antenna: Pr = PtG²λ²σ / ((4π)³R⁴). The wave crosses the path twice, so the echo falls as 1/R⁴ instead of the 1/R² of a one-way Friis link.

Watch for

  • Double R: the echo drops 12 dB. The one-way link drops only 6 dB.
  • Rmax ∝ (PtG²σ)1/4. Doubling the range needs 16 × the power, or 4 × the gain.
  • Gain counts twice because the same antenna transmits and receives. Each extra dB of G adds 2 dB of echo.
